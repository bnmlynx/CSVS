Clinical Research Skills & Critical Appraisal

A nine-module learning programme taking you through the full research journey — from identifying a question worth asking, through approvals, funding and analysis, to disseminating your findings. Aligned to the CSVS Research Series.

Module 1 of 9
Module 1

Identifying a Research Question

1.1

Where Good Research Questions Come From

Research starts with a genuine gap in knowledge — not with a dataset looking for a purpose. Collecting data first and asking questions afterwards produces underpowered, unfocused studies that struggle to gain approval or funding.

Productive sources of research questions include:

  • Clinical frustration — something in your practice that does not work as well as it should
  • Unexplained variation — why do outcomes differ between operators, sites or patient groups?
  • Guideline gaps — recommendations based on weak or absent evidence ("expert consensus")
  • New technology — a new imaging method or device that needs development and validation
  • Literature "future work" sections — authors telling you exactly what remains unknown
  • Patient and public involvement — what actually matters to the people receiving care

Ask yourself: if I answered this question tomorrow, what would change? If nothing would change for patients, services or the knowledge base, the question needs more work.

1.2

Is My Study Actually Research?

Before anything else, you must establish which of three project types you are proposing. This determines whether you need NHS Research Ethics Committee (REC) review, and getting it wrong can invalidate months of work.

 ResearchClinical AuditService Evaluation
Purpose To generate new knowledge To measure current practice against an agreed standard To measure the quality of care currently delivered
Findings are Generalisable (apply to a wider population) or transferable (apply to similar settings) Local — relevant to the service audited Local — relevant to the service evaluated
Standard No pre-existing standard; the study creates the evidence Measured against an existing standard or guideline No standard; describes what is currently done
Randomisation May involve randomisation Never Never
Treatment / care May allocate outside normal practice Normal practice only Normal practice only
NHS REC review Usually required Not required Not required

Important: "not research" does not mean "no governance". Audit and service evaluation still need local registration and approval through your Trust's clinical audit or R&D department, and must still comply with data protection law.

The HRA provides two short decision tools that give you a documented answer you can attach to your application:

  • HRA — "Is my study research?" Answers a short series of questions and tells you whether your project is research, audit, service evaluation, PPI/engagement or surveillance. Based on the Research Ethics Service "Defining Research" table.
  • HRA — "Do I need NHS REC review?" Once you know your project is research, this tool tells you whether it needs review by an NHS Research Ethics Committee.

1.3

The National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR)

The NIHR is the UK's largest funder of health and care research, investing around £1 billion each year. It is funded by the Department of Health and Social Care and exists to fund, enable and deliver research that improves health and care.

For someone identifying a research question, the NIHR matters in four ways:

  • It tells you what needs researching — the NIHR publishes identified evidence gaps and prioritised research needs, so you can align your question with a recognised national priority
  • It supports the James Lind Alliance — which produces the "Top 10" research priority lists (covered in Module 3)
  • It funds the work — through programmes such as Research for Patient Benefit, HTA and its Academy fellowships (covered in Module 7)
  • It helps you write the application — through the free NIHR Research Support Service

The NIHR Research Support Service (RSS)

The RSS replaced the former Research Design Service. It offers free, confidential advice to researchers in England at every stage from pre-application to post-award delivery, delivered through eight regional hubs. It explicitly welcomes early-career researchers, people new to research funding, and those without established research networks.

Practical tip: contact the RSS early — months before a funding deadline, while your question is still taking shape. Approaching them a fortnight before submission is the single most common mistake.

  • NIHR — main site Funding programmes, research news, and career development routes.
  • NIHR — Identify research needs How research needs and evidence gaps are identified and prioritised, including via James Lind Alliance Priority Setting Partnerships.
  • NIHR Research Support Service Free design and application support. Find your regional hub here.
  • NIHR — Funding opportunities Current open calls across all NIHR programmes.
1.4

The Health Research Authority (HRA)

The HRA is the body that protects and promotes the interests of patients and the public in health and social care research. Where the NIHR funds research, the HRA regulates and approves it.

The HRA's responsibilities include:

  • HRA Approval — the single approval required for all project-based research in the NHS in England and Wales
  • The Research Ethics Service — overseeing the network of Research Ethics Committees
  • IRAS — the Integrated Research Application System, the single online portal for research applications
  • Standards and guidance — including the UK Policy Framework for Health and Social Care Research, which is the document that formally defines what counts as research
  • Transparency — requiring registration of studies and publication of results

At the question-identification stage, use the HRA to sanity-check your project type and your approvals burden. A question that requires a CTIMP (a trial of an investigational medicinal product) carries a very different regulatory load from an observational imaging study — and that should inform whether the question is feasible for you. Module 5 covers the approvals route in full.

  • Health Research Authority Approvals, guidance, policy and standards for health research in the UK.
  • HRA — What approvals do I need? Maps your study type to the approvals you will have to obtain.
  • IRAS — Integrated Research Application System The single portal for REC, HRA and MHRA applications.
1.5

A Framework for Developing Your Question: FINER

Once you have a rough idea, the FINER criteria test whether it is worth pursuing before you invest in refining it into a formal question. Work through each criterion honestly — a question that fails on feasibility is not rescued by being interesting.

CriterionThe question to ask yourself
FeasibleDo I have access to enough patients, the time, the funding, the technical skill and the expertise? Can I realistically recruit the numbers a power calculation will demand?
InterestingIs this genuinely interesting to colleagues, patients and funders — not just to me?
NovelDoes it confirm, refute or extend existing findings? Has the literature review (Module 2) confirmed the gap is real?
EthicalWould a Research Ethics Committee grant a favourable opinion? Is the risk to participants proportionate to the knowledge gained?
RelevantWill it advance scientific knowledge, influence clinical practice or shape future research direction?

Module 2

Conducting a Literature Review

2.1

What Is a Literature Review?

A literature review is:

  • A systematic and reproducible search of the existing evidence base
  • A critical evaluation of sources — not just a summary
  • A thematic synthesis that identifies patterns and gaps
  • A justification for your own research question

Types of literature review:

  • Narrative — Broad overview, less structured
  • Systematic — Rigorous, reproducible methodology following protocols such as PRISMA
  • Scoping — Maps the extent and nature of evidence on a topic
  • Meta-analysis — Statistical combination of results from multiple studies

Link back to Module 1: the literature review is how you prove the "N" in FINER. Until you have searched properly, a gap in knowledge is only a gap in your knowledge.

2.2

Advanced Search Strategy

A rigorous search strategy should include:

  • Boolean operators — AND, OR, NOT to combine terms
  • MeSH terms — Controlled vocabulary for medical/health databases
  • Truncation and wildcards — e.g. vascul* to capture vascular, vasculature, vasculitis
  • Citation chaining — Following references forwards and backwards
  • Grey literature — Conference proceedings, theses, policy documents, trial registries
  • Documentation — Full recording of search strings, databases, dates and filters
Suggested Template: Search Strategy Log

For each search, record: database name, date searched, exact search string, filters applied, and number of results returned. Without this log your review is not reproducible — and a reviewer will ask for it.

  • PubMed Free access to MEDLINE. Use the MeSH database to build controlled-vocabulary searches.
  • Cochrane Library Systematic reviews and the CENTRAL register of controlled trials.
  • PRISMA Statement Reporting checklist and flow diagram for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
  • EThOS — British Library e-theses UK doctoral theses — a valuable grey-literature source often missed.
2.3

Synthesis at Postgraduate Level

Move beyond descriptive summary to analytical synthesis:

  • Thematic mapping — Group findings by theme, not by individual study
  • Methodological comparison — Compare strengths and weaknesses across studies
  • Gap justification — Clearly articulate what remains unknown
  • Theoretical tensions — Identify conflicting frameworks or findings

The commonest failure: a review that reads "Smith (2019) found X. Jones (2020) found Y. Patel (2021) found Z." That is an annotated bibliography, not a synthesis. Organise by theme, and make every paragraph carry an argument.

2.4

Critical Appraisal: Frameworks

Reading the literature is not the same as appraising it. Use established tools to systematically evaluate research quality:

  • CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme) — Checklists for different study types
  • Joanna Briggs Institute — Standardised tools for various methodologies
  • CONSORT — Reporting guidelines for randomised controlled trials (RCTs)
  • STROBE — Reporting guidelines for observational studies
  • QUADAS-2 — Quality assessment for diagnostic accuracy studies
  • CASP checklists Free appraisal checklists for RCTs, cohort studies, qualitative research, diagnostic studies and more.
  • EQUATOR Network The library of reporting guidelines — find the right one for any study design.
2.5

Critical Appraisal: Validity

Internal Validity — Can we trust the results within the study?

  • Bias — Selection, measurement, attrition, reporting bias
  • Confounding — Unmeasured variables influencing outcomes
  • Randomisation — Were groups allocated fairly, and was allocation concealed?
  • Blinding — Were participants, clinicians and assessors blinded where possible?

External Validity — Can findings be applied beyond the study?

  • Generalisability — Does the sample represent the wider population?
  • Population relevance — Are inclusion/exclusion criteria appropriate?
  • Setting — Would the result hold in a district general hospital as well as a tertiary centre?
2.6

Critical Appraisal: Advanced Critique

Postgraduate-level critique should go beyond basic checklists to assess:

  • Ontological coherence (qualitative) — Does the methodology align with the philosophical position?
  • Statistical assumptions — Were tests used appropriately for the data? (See Module 8)
  • Missing data handling — How were dropouts, non-responses and incomplete data treated?
  • Funding bias — Could the funding source or declared interests influence findings?
  • Reproducibility — Could another researcher replicate this study from the methods section alone?
  • Transparency — Was the protocol pre-registered? Does the published outcome match the registered primary outcome?

A fast, high-yield check: compare the paper's stated primary outcome against its trial registry entry. Silent outcome-switching is common and immediately tells you how much weight to give the headline result.

Research Question & Hypothesis

3.1

The PICO Model

Module 1 established whether your question is worth asking. PICO makes it answerable. It forces you to specify four elements — and an inability to fill in any one of them is a sign the question is not yet ready.

ElementMeaningExample
P — PopulationWho are you studying? Be specific about age, condition, severity and setting.Adults with ≥70% asymptomatic carotid stenosis
I — InterventionWhat are you doing, testing, measuring or exposing them to?Carotid endarterectomy
C — Comparison / ControlWhat are you comparing it against? This may be usual care, placebo, another test, or nothing.Best medical therapy alone
O — OutcomeWhat will you measure, how, and when? Outcomes should matter to patients, not just be easy to record.Ipsilateral stroke or death within 5 years

Variations on PICO

  • PICOT / PICOTS — adds Time frame and Setting
  • PICOS — adds Study design, useful when framing a systematic review
  • PEO — Population, Exposure, Outcome — for observational studies with no intervention
  • SPIDER — Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type — for qualitative questions

Watch the outcome. "Improved patient outcomes" is not an outcome. "Change in maximum walking distance on a standardised treadmill test at 6 months" is. If you cannot state how and when you will measure it, you cannot power the study or analyse it.

3.3

Three Types of Quantitative Research Question

Quantitative research questions fall into three families. Knowing which one you are asking determines your study design (Module 4) and your statistical test (Module 8), so decide this deliberately rather than by accident.

1. Descriptive — "how much?"

Describes what is happening: the frequency, magnitude or distribution of a variable in a defined group. No comparison, no relationship — just measurement.

  • What is the prevalence of asymptomatic carotid stenosis in patients over 65 attending a vascular clinic?
  • How accurately does duplex ultrasound predict stroke risk in carotid disease?

2. Relational — "is there an association?"

Explores the relationship between two or more variables (a factor, trait or condition). Note that association is not causation — a relational question tells you variables move together, not that one causes the other.

  • What is the relationship between carotid artery disease and cognitive decline?
  • Is there an association between ankle-brachial pressure index and 5-year cardiovascular mortality?

3. Comparative — "what is the difference?"

Compares two or more groups on an outcome. This is the family that most readily converts into a testable hypothesis.

  • What is the difference in stroke rates between patients managed medically and those undergoing carotid endarterectomy?
  • Does supervised exercise therapy improve walking distance more than unsupervised advice in claudication?
  • Laerd — How to structure quantitative research questions Detailed, worked guidance on phrasing descriptive, relational and comparative questions, with sentence templates for each. Highly recommended when drafting.
3.4

Top 10 Priorities for Research

Before finalising your question, check whether it already appears on a published priority list. A question that maps onto a recognised national priority is substantially easier to justify to a Research Ethics Committee and far more competitive for funding.

The James Lind Alliance (JLA)

Established in 2003 and supported by the NIHR, the JLA runs Priority Setting Partnerships (PSPs). These bring together patients, carers and health professionals as equal partners to identify and prioritise the evidence uncertainties that matter most to them. Each PSP publishes a "Top 10" list of unanswered questions.

The process is deliberately structured:

Establish the partnershipA steering group of patients, carers and clinicians, with project partners.
Gather uncertaintiesOpen survey asking what questions people want answered.
Verify against the evidenceCheck that each submitted question is genuinely unanswered by existing research.
Interim prioritisationA wider survey reduces the long list to around 25 questions.
Final workshopPatients, carers and clinicians agree the final Top 10 by consensus.

Over 150 PSPs have now published priorities, spanning treatment, cause, diagnosis, prognosis and social care. Funders take them seriously: the Stroke Association, for example, explicitly requires project grant applications to address the JLA Top 10 for stroke.

  • JLA — Top 10 priorities The full searchable index of published Top 10 lists across all completed Priority Setting Partnerships.
  • JLA — Vascular conditions Top 10s Priorities specific to vascular disease, agreed by patients, carers and clinicians.
  • James Lind Alliance How PSPs work, and how to propose one in an area with no existing list.
3.5

From Question to Hypothesis

A research question asks; a hypothesis predicts. A hypothesis is a specific, testable statement about the expected relationship between variables, written before data collection begins.

Hypotheses come in a pair:

  • Null hypothesis (H₀) — states there is no difference, effect or association. This is the statement you actually test.
  • Alternative hypothesis (H₁) — states there is a difference, effect or association.

Worked example.
H₀: Drug X does not reduce the amplitude of brain blood pulsations.
H₁: Drug X reduces the amplitude of brain blood pulsations.

Note carefully what a statistical test does: it assesses the probability of observing your data if the null hypothesis were true. You therefore reject or fail to reject H₀. You never "prove" H₁.

Language matters. "We failed to reject the null hypothesis" is not the same as "we proved there is no effect." Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — particularly in an underpowered study.

3.6

Deductive and Inductive Reasoning

 Deductive reasoningInductive reasoning
DirectionTheory → hypothesis → observation → confirmationObservation → pattern → tentative hypothesis → theory
Starts withAn existing theory or body of evidenceSpecific observations or data
Typical ofQuantitative research, hypothesis testing, RCTsQualitative research, exploratory and grounded-theory work
ProducesA test of whether a prediction holdsNew theory generated from the data

Most quantitative clinical research is deductive: you draw on existing theory to predict what should happen, state that prediction as a hypothesis, then design a study capable of showing you are wrong.

Popper and falsification

Karl Popper argued that science advances not by proving theories true but by attempting to falsify them. No number of confirming observations can prove a universal claim, but a single disconfirming observation can refute it. This is precisely why research tests the null hypothesis: the null is the statement that can be decisively disproven.

The practical consequence for your study design is uncomfortable but important: design the study that could show you are wrong. A design that can only ever confirm what you already believe is not a test of anything.

Pre-registration. Stating your hypothesis and primary outcome publicly before data collection — on a trial registry or a platform such as the OSF — is the strongest available protection against unconsciously shifting your hypothesis to fit your results.

Module 4

Research Methodologies

4.1

Letting the Question Drive the Method

Methodology is not a matter of preference. The question type you settled on in Module 3 largely determines the design you need:

Your question asks...Typical design
How much / how many? (descriptive)Cross-sectional study, survey, diagnostic accuracy study
Is there an association? (relational)Cohort study, case-control study, correlational analysis
What is the difference? (comparative)Randomised controlled trial, quasi-experimental or comparative cohort study
What is this like / why? (exploratory)Qualitative — interviews, focus groups, ethnography

A common error: choosing an RCT because it sits at the top of the evidence hierarchy, when the question is actually descriptive. The "best" design is the one that answers your question, not the one with the highest status.

4.2

Quantitative Research

Characteristics of quantitative approaches:

  • Hypothesis testing — Starts with a testable prediction
  • Structured design — Predetermined variables and procedures
  • Large samples — Aims for statistical power and generalisability
  • Causality — Experimental designs can establish cause and effect
  • Deductive — Moves from theory to data (Module 3)
4.3

Qualitative Research

Characteristics of qualitative approaches:

  • Meaning exploration — Seeks to understand experiences and perspectives
  • Interviews and focus groups — In-depth data collection methods
  • Thematic analysis — Identifying patterns and themes in the data
  • Reflexivity — The researcher acknowledges their influence on the research
  • Data saturation — Sampling continues until no new themes emerge, rather than to a pre-set n
  • Inductive — Moves from data to theory (Module 3)

Mixed methods

Mixed-methods designs combine both, and are increasingly common in health services research — for example an RCT with an embedded qualitative process evaluation explaining why an intervention did or did not work.

4.4

Study Design Hierarchy

The hierarchy of evidence (from strongest to weakest for questions of effectiveness):

  1. Systematic Review / Meta-analysis
  2. Randomised Controlled Trial (RCT)
  3. Cohort Study
  4. Case-Control Study
  5. Cross-Sectional Study
  6. Case Series / Case Report
  7. Expert Opinion

Read the hierarchy carefully. It ranks designs for questions of effectiveness. For a question about prevalence, a well-conducted cross-sectional study beats an RCT; for a question about lived experience, a qualitative study beats both.

Module 5

Approvals for Clinical Research

5.1

Why This Module Matters

The approvals process is where well-conceived studies most often stall. For a complex study it can take over six months, and errors mean going back to the start of a queue. Build the timeline into your project plan from the outset, and get it right first time.

Absolute rule: you may not recruit participants, access identifiable data, or collect research data until every required approval is in place and your sponsor has given you written authorisation to begin. Data collected before approval generally cannot be used.

5.2

Step 1: Confirm Your Project Is Research

Return to the HRA decision tools from Module 1. If your project is audit or service evaluation it does not need REC review — but it does need local registration. Keep the tool's output as documentary evidence of your decision.

  • HRA — "Is my study research?" Establishes whether your project is research, audit, service evaluation, PPI or surveillance.
  • HRA — "Do I need NHS REC review?" Determines whether NHS REC review is required for your research project.
5.3

Step 2: Secure a Sponsor

Every study needs a sponsor — the organisation that takes formal responsibility for the initiation, management and financing of the research. This is usually your NHS Trust, a university, or a commercial company.

The sponsor must be in place before you submit anything, because they:

  • Approve the protocol and confirm it is scientifically sound
  • Provide indemnity and insurance cover
  • Take responsibility for governance, monitoring and reporting
  • Authorise the study to begin once all approvals are obtained (the "green light")

Start here. Your Trust R&D department is your single most valuable resource throughout this whole module. Approach them at the protocol stage, not at the submission stage.

5.4

Step 3: Prepare Your Documents

You will need a complete document set before you can submit. Your R&D department will usually hold templates.

  • Protocol — the full study plan: background, objectives, design, population, outcomes, analysis plan
  • Participant Information Sheet (PIS) — written in plain English for the target population
  • Consent form
  • Case Report Forms (CRFs) — how you will record data
  • GP/clinician letters, invitation letters, advertising material
  • Schedule of Events and Cost Attribution Template (SoECAT) — a spreadsheet downloaded via IRAS that attributes every activity and cost as NHS Support, NHS Treatment or Research cost. It must be signed off by an AcoRD specialist in your Clinical Research Network.
  • Organisation Information Document (OID) — sets out commercial/non-commercial status and the delegation log of authorised staff; it acts as the sponsor-to-site agreement
  • Intellectual property strategy — covering background, foreground, sideground and postground IP. Involve your Trust's IP specialists early if a device or method may be commercialised.

On the PIS: RECs comment on participant information sheets more than on any other document. Have a lay reader — ideally a PPI representative — read it before you submit.

5.5

Step 4: Apply Through IRAS

IRAS (the Integrated Research Application System) is the single online portal for research applications in the UK. You complete one dataset which is then used to generate applications to the REC, the HRA, and where relevant the MHRA.

For clinical trials of investigational medicinal products, combined review means a single application produces a coordinated MHRA and REC decision.

  • IRAS The application portal. It has a built-in help function alongside every question — use it.
  • HRA — What approvals do I need? Maps your study type to the specific approvals required.
5.6

Step 5: REC Review and HRA Approval

This is the part most often misunderstood, so be precise about the terminology:

ApprovalWho grants itWhat it covers
REC favourable opinion An independent Research Ethics Committee, via the HRA's Research Ethics Service The ethical acceptability of the study: risk to participants, consent, information, confidentiality, vulnerable groups
HRA Approval HRA assessment staff Regulatory compliance and study-wide governance: legal basis for data processing, contracts, sponsorship, suitability
Capacity & capability Each participating NHS organisation, individually Whether that site actually has the staff, facilities and resources to deliver the study

A single IRAS application triggers both the REC review and the HRA assessment. HRA Approval and a REC favourable opinion together allow the study to proceed nationally — but no site may start until it has separately confirmed capacity and capability.

What the REC can decide

  • Favourable opinion — you may proceed (sometimes with conditions)
  • Provisional opinion — further information or changes requested; you have up to 60 calendar days to respond
  • Unfavourable opinion — the study may not proceed as submitted; there is a right of appeal

Depending on the risk profile, review may be by a proportionate review subcommittee (faster, for studies with no material ethical issues) or by a full committee meeting, which you will be invited to attend. Attending is strongly advised — you can answer questions directly and often resolve issues that would otherwise become conditions.

  • HRA — Applying to a Research Ethics Committee The REC process end to end, including booking a review and what happens at the meeting.
  • HRA Approval What HRA Approval covers and how it relates to REC opinion and site capacity and capability.
5.7

Step 6: Additional Approvals You May Need

  • MHRA authorisation — required for Clinical Trials of Investigational Medicinal Products (CTIMPs) and certain device trials. Assessment normally takes around 30 days but can extend considerably.
  • Human Tissue Authority (HTA) — where relevant human tissue is stored or used; Tissue Transfer Agreements are needed between sites.
  • NIHR portfolio adoption — in England this happens automatically on submission via the Research Delivery Network; in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland contact the regional network directly. Portfolio adoption unlocks NHS support costs and research nurse time.
  • National programme approval — research involving a national screening programme (for example the NHS Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Screening Programme) needs separate approval from its Research Advisory Committee.
  • Research passport / letter of access — if you will be working at a Trust that does not employ you.
  • Data protection — a Data Protection Impact Assessment and a documented lawful basis under UK GDPR; the HRA assessment checks this.
5.8

Step 7: Green Light, and Life During the Study

All approvals obtainedHRA Approval, REC favourable opinion, and MHRA authorisation if applicable.
Site confirms capacity and capabilityEach participating NHS organisation confirms it can deliver the study.
Sponsor green-lightWritten authorisation from your sponsor that you may now begin. Keep this email.
Site file completeStore every approval, version-controlled document and delegation log in the investigator site file.
RecruitOnly now may the first participant be approached.

Approval is not the end of the process. During the study you must:

  • Submit annual progress reports to the sponsor, REC and MHRA where applicable
  • Submit any amendment for approval before implementing it — including changes to the protocol, documents or research team
  • Report serious adverse events and serious breaches within required timeframes
  • Submit an end of study declaration, and a final report, within the required period after completion

Where to get help: your Trust R&D department first, then the IRAS in-application help, the HRA's own guidance pages, and the NIHR Research Support Service. The CSVS Research Committee can also be contacted for advice on vascular science studies.

Module 6

Ethics in Clinical Research

6.1

Ethical Foundations

Core principles derived from the Declaration of Helsinki, developed by the World Medical Association:

  • Autonomy — Respect for participants' right to make informed decisions
  • Beneficence — Research should aim to do good
  • Non-maleficence — Minimise harm and risk to participants
  • Justice — Fair distribution of research benefits and burdens

In the UK these are given practical force by the UK Policy Framework for Health and Social Care Research, which sets out the responsibilities of sponsors, investigators and organisations.

Ethics and approvals are related but distinct. Module 5 covered the process of obtaining a REC favourable opinion. This module covers the reasoning a committee applies — and that you should apply to your own design long before you submit.

6.2

Informed Consent

Valid consent has three components, all of which must be present:

  • Capacity — the person is able to understand, retain, weigh and communicate a decision
  • Information — they have been given sufficient information, in a form they can understand, including the right to withdraw
  • Voluntariness — the decision is free from coercion or undue influence

Consent is a process, not a signature. Participants need adequate time to consider, an opportunity to ask questions, and the ability to withdraw at any point without their care being affected.

Where consent gets complicated: adults lacking capacity, children and young people, emergency and unconscious patients, and research on tissue or data where re-consent is impractical. Each has a specific legal route — take R&D advice rather than improvising.

6.3

Ethics Committees

Research Ethics Committees are independent bodies, including both expert and lay members, responsible for:

  • Risk-benefit analysis — Weighing potential harm against expected benefits
  • Consent process review — Ensuring participants give truly informed consent
  • Participant information — Checking documents are clear, honest and readable
  • Confidentiality safeguards — Protecting participant data and identity
  • Data protection compliance — Ensuring adherence to UK GDPR and institutional policies
  • Protection of vulnerable groups — Applying additional safeguards where needed

Crucially, a REC also considers scientific quality as an ethical issue: a study too poorly designed to answer its question exposes participants to inconvenience or risk for no possible benefit, and is therefore unethical regardless of how safe it is.

6.4

Data Protection and Confidentiality

  • Lawful basis — under UK GDPR, health research by a public body normally relies on "public task" plus the research condition, not on consent as the lawful basis. Consent for participation and lawful basis for processing are two different things.
  • Anonymisation vs pseudonymisation — pseudonymised data can still be re-identified via a key and remains personal data; truly anonymised data does not.
  • Data minimisation — collect only what your analysis plan requires
  • Storage and retention — secure, on approved systems, for a defined and justified period
  • Transfer — data sharing agreements for any transfer outside the sponsor organisation
6.5

Contemporary Ethical Challenges

  • Dual roles — researching your own patients or students creates power imbalances that threaten voluntariness
  • Vulnerable populations — additional protections and sensitivity required
  • Incidental findings — what will you do if a research scan reveals unexpected pathology? Decide, and tell participants, before it happens
  • AI in research — transparency about AI-assisted analysis or writing, and the data governance implications of uploading participant data to third-party tools
  • Research integrity — fabrication, falsification, plagiarism, selective reporting and gift authorship
  • Equity of access — are your inclusion criteria and recruitment routes systematically excluding groups who would benefit?
  • UK Policy Framework for Health and Social Care Research The principles and responsibilities governing all health and social care research in the UK.
  • WMA Declaration of Helsinki The founding statement of ethical principles for medical research involving human participants.

Statistics for Researchers

NIHR Statistics Group →

8.1

Descriptive vs Inferential Statistics

Descriptive statistics summarise your data:

  • Mean — The average value
  • Median — The middle value in ordered data; preferred for skewed distributions
  • Standard deviation — How spread out values are from the mean
  • Interquartile range — The spread of the middle 50%, reported alongside the median

Inferential statistics draw conclusions beyond your sample:

  • p-value — The probability of observing results at least as extreme as yours if the null hypothesis were true
  • Confidence intervals — A range of plausible values for the true effect
  • Hypothesis testing — The formal process of rejecting or failing to reject the null hypothesis (Module 3)
8.2

Types of Data

Your data type determines which test is valid — get this wrong and everything downstream is invalid.

TypeDescriptionExample
NominalCategories with no orderBlood group; sex; treatment arm
OrdinalOrdered categories, unequal intervalsRutherford category; pain score 1–5
Interval / ratioContinuous numerical dataPeak systolic velocity; walking distance; age

Also check whether your data are parametric (approximately normally distributed, with comparable variance) or non-parametric. Parametric tests are more powerful but only valid when their assumptions hold.

8.3

Beyond p < 0.05

Statistical significance alone is not enough. Consider:

  • Effect size (e.g. Cohen's d) — How large is the effect in practical terms?
  • Confidence intervals — How precise is the estimate? A wide CI crossing the line of no effect tells you far more than a p-value alone
  • Clinical significance — Does the effect exceed the minimum clinically important difference?
  • Sample size influence — Very large samples produce statistically significant but trivially small effects
  • Multiple testing — Testing twenty outcomes at p < 0.05 will on average produce one "significant" result by chance alone

Type I and Type II errors

 H₀ is actually trueH₀ is actually false
You reject H₀Type I error (α) — false positiveCorrect decision
You fail to reject H₀Correct decisionType II error (β) — false negative

Statistical power (1 − β) is the probability of detecting an effect that is really there, conventionally set at 80% or 90%. An underpowered study risks a Type II error — and, as Module 6 noted, wastes participants' time for no possible benefit.

Involve a statistician at the design stage, not the analysis stage. Consulting one after data collection often amounts, in R.A. Fisher's phrase, to asking for a post-mortem examination.

Dissemination of Research

9.1

Why Dissemination Is an Obligation

Research that is never reported has wasted participants' time, consumed NHS resources and contributed nothing to the evidence base. Non-publication is not a neutral outcome — it actively distorts the literature, because studies with negative or null results are the ones most often left unpublished.

Dissemination is also a formal requirement, not a courtesy:

  • The UK Policy Framework for Health and Social Care Research requires findings to be made accessible
  • The HRA requires research to be registered and results published, and monitors compliance
  • The Declaration of Helsinki obliges researchers to make results publicly available, including negative and inconclusive findings
  • Most funders make publication and open access a condition of the award
  • You almost certainly promised participants you would share the findings — in your participant information sheet

Publication bias. When null results stay in the file drawer, systematic reviews systematically overestimate treatment effects. Publishing a well-conducted negative study is a genuine scientific contribution.

9.2

Plan Dissemination Before You Start

A dissemination plan is required in most grant applications and ethics submissions, so write it at the protocol stage. It should answer:

  • Who needs to know? Participants, clinicians, commissioners, policymakers, patient organisations, the public
  • What does each audience need? A peer-reviewed paper serves none of these groups equally well
  • Through what channel? Journal, conference, professional network, patient charity, guideline group
  • When? Interim outputs, primary results, secondary analyses
  • Who is responsible? Named individuals with timescales — otherwise it does not happen

Agree authorship at the start. Authorship disputes are among the most damaging and avoidable problems in research teams. Write down the expected order and the criteria while everyone is still optimistic.

9.3

Conferences and Abstracts

Conferences are usually the first route to disseminate, and they are valuable well beyond the presentation itself — the questions you get are free peer review before you commit to a manuscript.

  • Oral presentation — the highest-profile route; usually competitive selection from submitted abstracts
  • Poster presentation — excellent for discussion; design for legibility at two metres, not for completeness
  • Rapid-fire / short communication — increasingly common hybrid formats

Relevant meetings for vascular science include the CSVS Annual Scientific Meeting, BMUS Annual Scientific Meeting, the Vascular Societies' Annual Scientific Meeting, and the UK Stroke Forum.

Abstract discipline: a structured abstract (background, methods, results, conclusion) with actual numbers beats a vague one every time. Never submit "results will be presented" — reviewers score it poorly and some meetings reject it outright.

9.4

Peer-Reviewed Publication

Choosing a journal

  • Scope — read recent issues; would your paper look at home there? Mis-targeting causes most desk rejections
  • Audience — a specialist journal read by the people who would change practice may serve you better than a higher-impact general journal
  • Impact factor — one crude metric among several; do not let it override reaching the right readership
  • Time to decision and acceptance rate — usually published on the journal website
  • Open access policy and article processing charges — check whether your funder covers these

Predatory journals. If you receive a flattering unsolicited email with a rapid-publication promise and a fee, be suspicious. Check whether the journal is indexed in PubMed/MEDLINE, whether it is listed in the Directory of Open Access Journals, whether the editorial board is real and contactable, and whether peer review is genuinely described. Publishing in a predatory journal buries your work and damages your record — and it cannot usually be undone.

The peer review process

Submission and editorial triageMany papers are desk-rejected here, usually for scope rather than quality.
External peer reviewTypically two or three reviewers; weeks to months.
DecisionAccept (rare first time), minor revision, major revision, or reject.
Response to reviewersAnswer every point explicitly and courteously, even where you disagree. Explain your reasoning rather than ignoring the comment.
Acceptance and proofsCheck proofs carefully — errors introduced at this stage are your responsibility.

A rejection is not a verdict on the work. Reviewer comments are usually the most detailed expert feedback you will ever get on it — use them and submit elsewhere.

9.6

Authorship

The ICMJE criteria are the accepted international standard. An author must meet all four:

  1. Substantial contribution to conception or design, or to acquisition, analysis or interpretation of data
  2. Drafting the work or revising it critically for important intellectual content
  3. Final approval of the version to be published
  4. Agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work

Contributors who do not meet all four should be named in the acknowledgements instead.

Practices to avoid: gift authorship (adding a senior colleague who made no contribution), ghost authorship (omitting someone who did, often an industry writer), and coercive authorship. All are recognised breaches of research integrity.

  • ICMJE Recommendations Authorship criteria, conflicts of interest, and standards for reporting medical research.
9.7

Open Science

  • Open access — Gold (published open access, usually for a fee), Green (author-accepted manuscript deposited in a repository), or Diamond (free to both author and reader). Most UK public funders now mandate immediate open access.
  • Preprints — posting a manuscript to a server such as medRxiv before peer review establishes priority and speeds dissemination. Most journals permit it; check first, and always label it clearly as not peer reviewed.
  • Trial registration — prospective registration on ISRCTN or ClinicalTrials.gov is required by the HRA and by ICMJE journals as a condition of publication
  • Data sharing — follow the FAIR principles: Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable. Ensure your consent form and ethics approval permit sharing, and share only appropriately anonymised data.
  • ORCID — a persistent identifier that reliably links your outputs to you
  • ISRCTN Registry UK trial registration.
  • medRxiv Preprint server for health sciences.
  • Directory of Open Access Journals Use it to check that an open access journal is legitimate.
9.8

Beyond the Academic Paper

A journal article reaches researchers. It rarely reaches the people whose care you were trying to improve. Plan at least one non-academic output:

  • Feedback to participants — a plain-English summary sent to everyone who took part. You promised this; it costs little and matters enormously.
  • Plain-English summaries — required by NIHR and most funders; also the version that gets read by commissioners
  • Patient organisations and charities — newsletters and websites with far larger readerships than most journals
  • Professional networks — CSVS and BMUS newsletters, regional meetings, journal clubs
  • Guidelines and policy — submit evidence to NICE consultations and guideline development groups; this is where research actually changes practice
  • Departmental teaching and local implementation — the shortest path from finding to changed practice
  • Media and social media — effective for reach, but check your institution's communications policy and never overstate the findings

Close the loop. The research cycle ends where it began: your findings, and the new uncertainties they raise, become someone else's Module 1. Say explicitly in your discussion what should be studied next.
